Traveling for work goes more smoothly when a few practical details are handled early, and this short guide collects the advice our frequent travelers share most often. It is deliberately written as flowing prose rather than a tidy list, because real-world source material — scraped web pages, emailed guidance, PDF handbooks — usually looks like this, with no clean headings to split on.

Entry requirements are the thing people forget until it is too late. Most business trips to the Schengen area allow stays of up to ninety days within any one hundred and eighty day period without a visa for many passport holders, but this is not universal, so always confirm against your own nationality before booking. A passport should have at least six months of validity remaining beyond your planned return date, and several countries will deny boarding if it does not. If your trip involves a layover in a third country, check whether that country requires a transit visa, because some do even when you never leave the airport. Keep a digital and a paper copy of any visa approval, and store your passport number somewhere separate from the passport itself in case it is lost.

Packing for a short business trip is mostly about avoiding checked luggage. A cabin bag that fits the strictest airline limit you might encounter saves both time and baggage fees, and it removes the risk of a lost suitcase before an important meeting. Liquids in carry-on must be in containers of one hundred milliliters or less, gathered in a single transparent resealable bag, and a spare set of clothes in your cabin bag is wise even for a one-night trip. Chargers, adapters, and a small power bank belong in the cabin bag too, since airlines increasingly forbid loose lithium batteries in the hold.

Jet lag is worth planning around when you cross more than three time zones. The most effective habit is to shift your sleep schedule toward the destination time a day or two before departure, and then to adopt local meal and sleep times immediately on arrival rather than clinging to home time. Daylight exposure in the morning helps your body clock move forward, while bright light late in the evening pushes it back, so use light deliberately. Staying hydrated and going easy on alcohol and heavy meals on the flight makes the first day noticeably easier.

Getting from the airport to the hotel is where costs and stress quietly add up. Research the options before you land: a prepaid train or metro is almost always cheaper and often faster than a taxi in major cities, and knowing the route in advance means you are not negotiating a fare while exhausted. If you do take a taxi or ride-hailing car, confirm it is a licensed vehicle and, where possible, agree the fare or use the app estimate rather than an open meter in an unfamiliar city. Allow generous buffer time for the return trip to the airport, because traffic and security lines are the two most common reasons people miss flights.

Travel insurance and payment logistics round out the list. For international trips, insurance that covers medical treatment and trip interruption is inexpensive relative to the cost it protects against, and many corporate cards include a basic policy that travelers never think to use. Carry a small amount of local currency for places that do not take cards, but rely mainly on a card with no foreign transaction fee, and notify your bank of the trip so a legitimate purchase abroad is not flagged as fraud. Finally, the cheapest fares for both flights and hotels tend to appear several weeks ahead rather than at the last minute, so booking early is both a policy preference and simply a way to spend less.
